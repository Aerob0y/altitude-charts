Growing like donuts

Auckland's population growth is spreading towards the edges rather than building up around the centre.

Closeness offers connection, productivity, and opportunity. Being in a city means access to bars and restaurants, jobs, culture, and music. The demand for space within the city creates high rents and smaller living spaces. The advantages of cities pull us in, while the problems they create push us out. This push and pull balances cities into the shapes we expect: tall in the centre, falling away to mid-rises and suburban sprawl. But not in Auckland.

At first glance, the distribution of people may look normal.

At second glance, it's really not.

Auckland is a flat city. Outside the central city apartments, the landscape quickly gives way to houses, with little change in density from 4 to 14 km. Density only really drops off after Long Bay to the north and Flat Bush to the southeast, before rising again around Manukau and Papakura.

And it's getting flatter. Auckland's fringes have exploded in population, while the inner suburbs have barely grown at all. The inner suburbs, 2 to 6 km from Britomart, were home to 15% of Aucklanders in 2013 but took just 2% of the people added by 2023. Beyond 18 km held 13% of residents and took 38%.

Plotting that, we can see that most of the growth happened well away from the city. The further from the city, the faster it grew: the inner suburbs grew by 0.2% a year, against 3.5% a year beyond 18 km.

To measure that change in shape, we can use a Gini coefficient. If every suburb had grown at the same rate, the line would follow the diagonal. The further it sags below, the more growth went to the edges. On that scale 0 is even growth, and Auckland scores 0.37.

There could be a few reasons for this. Maybe there's a maximum height people don't want to build beyond. Maybe the desire for a house is so strong that we'd rather live in a standalone house in Pukekohe than a mid-rise in Ponsonby.

Auckland is turning into a donut-shaped city. In my view, that means longer commutes, less connection with others, lagging productivity, and missing out on the fun things that cities have to offer. We can do things differently.

Source: Stats NZ 2013 and 2023 Census usually resident population counts, Auckland urban area (2023 Census population change by statistical area 2).
Units: people, people per hectare, shares of residents and growth, and average yearly growth in percent.
Data covers: March 2013 and March 2023 Censuses.
