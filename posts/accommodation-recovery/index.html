A better year for accommodation, led by international guests and hotels

Guest nights and occupancy rose over the past year, but the gains came from overseas visitors, a few destinations and one type of accommodation.

In the 12 months to September 2026, New Zealand's commercial accommodation recorded 3.8% more guest nights than in the 12 months before. That headline hides a split. International guest nights rose in every region group except Wellington, while domestic guest nights fell in most of the country.

Auckland led, with international guest nights up 15.1% and domestic up 9.0%. Canterbury's international nights rose 12.6%, and Queenstown and Wanaka's 9.4%. Wellington was the exception, down on both counts.

Occupancy rose most where demand was strongest

More guests only lifts occupancy if capacity grows more slowly. Over the year, Queenstown and Wanaka's occupancy rose 5.5 percentage points to 73.6%, and Auckland's 4.4 points to 68.5%. In Wellington and the rest of the North Island, occupancy was flat or slightly lower.

Hotels took most of the growth

Almost all of the extra demand went to hotels. Hotel guest nights rose 9.1%, about 1.36 million of the 1.50 million extra guest nights nationally. Backpackers rose 6.1%. Holiday parks fell 1.0%, and motels and apartments were flat. Hotel occupancy rose 3.4 percentage points, which suggests demand grew faster than hotel capacity.

A longer view, and why it is hard to plan with

Looking back ten years, the chart has a hole in it. Stats NZ's Accommodation Survey ended in September 2019, and MBIE's Accommodation Data Programme started in June 2020. MBIE says the two are not directly comparable: they cover different properties, measure capacity differently and define accommodation types differently.

That matters for anyone planning capacity. An airport, a tourism organisation or a hotel investor wants a long, consistent series to judge whether today's demand is high or low. Here, the pre-COVID level and today's level come from different measures, so the question "are we back to 2019?" has no clean answer from this data. The past year's growth is solid. How it compares with before COVID is a judgement, not a number.
